**Book:** *Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die*  
**Authors:** Chip Heath and Dan Heath  
**Original publication:** 2007  
**Book sequence:** 05  
**Purpose:** Deep study notes for AI retrieval, reasoning, communication design, persuasion, teaching, marketing, leadership, and content creation.

# 1. BOOK OVERVIEW

*Made to Stick* asks a practical communication question:

**Why do some ideas remain in people's minds and influence what they do, while other ideas disappear almost immediately?**

The Heath brothers identify six recurring characteristics of sticky ideas:

1. **Simple**
2. **Unexpected**
3. **Concrete**
4. **Credible**
5. **Emotional**
6. **Stories**

Together these are commonly remembered as **SUCCESs**.

The authors also identify a major obstacle:

**The Curse of Knowledge** — once people know something, it becomes difficult for them to reconstruct what it feels like not to know it.

The book's central promise is not that great communication depends purely on natural charisma or creativity. It argues that communicators can deliberately design ideas to improve understanding, memory, and action. The authors' own site describes the book as explaining the anatomy of ideas that stick and highlights techniques including violating schemas, the Velcro Theory of Memory, and curiosity gaps. citeturn0search7turn0search14

# 2. WHAT DOES "STICKY" MEAN?

A sticky idea is not merely:

- short
- catchy
- entertaining
- repeated frequently

A useful operational definition is:

**An idea is sticky when people can understand it, remember it, and use or act on it later.**

Therefore:

**Stickiness = comprehension + memory + behavioral usefulness**

An idea can be memorable without being useful.

An idea can be useful without being remembered.

A strong sticky idea tries to improve all three.

---

# 3. THE SIX-PART SUCCESs MODEL

| Principle | Primary job |
|---|---|
| Simple | Find the core |
| Unexpected | Capture and maintain attention |
| Concrete | Make the idea understandable and memorable |
| Credible | Make people believe it |
| Emotional | Make people care |
| Stories | Help people act and mentally rehearse |

The principles are complementary.

A simple idea without credibility may be dismissed.

A credible idea without emotion may be ignored.

An emotional idea without concreteness may be misunderstood.

A concrete idea without simplicity may become overloaded.

A story without a useful core may entertain without teaching.

---

# 4. THE CURSE OF KNOWLEDGE

This is one of the most important concepts in the entire book.

## Definition

Once you know something, it becomes difficult to imagine what it was like before you knew it.

Experts therefore tend to communicate using:

- jargon
- assumptions
- abstractions
- compressed explanations
- specialist vocabulary
- missing intermediate steps

To the expert, the message feels obvious.

To the beginner, it feels incomplete or confusing.

---

# 5. TAP-PER AND LISTENER PROBLEM

The book uses a communication analogy involving a person who knows a song and another person who must guess it from rhythmic tapping.

The person tapping hears the complete song internally.

The listener hears only the taps.

This illustrates the core communication problem:

**The sender has information the receiver does not have.**

The sender therefore overestimates how much information the receiver is receiving.

### AI rule

When explaining something:

Do not ask:

**"Does this make sense to me?"**

Ask:

**"What can the audience infer from the information I actually gave them?"**

---

# 6. CURSE-OF-KNOWLEDGE DIAGNOSTIC

Before communicating, identify:

- What does the audience already know?
- What terminology do they understand?
- What assumptions am I making?
- What background information is missing?
- Which concepts are abstract?
- What could be misunderstood?
- What examples would make this visible?

Then adapt the message.

# 7. PRINCIPLE 1 — SIMPLE

## Core objective

**Find the essential core of the idea and communicate it compactly.**

Simple does not mean simplistic.

The goal is not to remove all complexity from reality.

The goal is to identify:

**What is the one thing people most need to remember?**

---

# 8. CORE VS. SUMMARY

A summary contains multiple points.

A core contains the organizing principle.

Example:

Weak summary:

- low price
- efficient operations
- fewer amenities
- high aircraft utilization
- simple routes

Potential core:

**"We compete by making air travel inexpensive."**

The details support the core.

---

# 9. PRIORITIZATION

The difficult part of simplicity is exclusion.

When everything is important:

**nothing is prioritized.**

A communicator must decide:

- What must be remembered?
- What can be omitted?
- What supports the core?
- What distracts from the core?

### AI rule

If a user asks for a message to be simplified:

1. Extract every claim.
2. Identify the desired outcome.
3. Rank claims by necessity.
4. Keep the core.
5. Remove secondary material.
6. Rebuild supporting detail around the core.

---

# 10. COMMANDER'S INTENT

A powerful model for simplicity is the idea of a concise statement that tells people the intended outcome of an operation.

The deeper principle:

**When detailed instructions cannot anticipate every situation, communicate the governing intent.**

This allows people to make local decisions without losing alignment.

### Business application

Instead of:

> Follow these 18 rules exactly.

Give:

**Objective + priority + boundary**

Then allow appropriate adaptation.

---

# 11. SIMPLE = CORE + COMPACT

A useful derived equation:

**Simple message = essential core + compact expression**

Not:

**Simple = fewer words**

A short message can still be complicated.

A simple message contains a clear organizing idea.

---

# 12. PROVERB STRUCTURE

Proverbs are useful because they compress broad experience into memorable language.

A strong principle often has:

- few words
- strong imagery
- broad applicability
- an implicit rule
- memorable rhythm

The AI lesson:

**Look for the proverb hidden inside a complicated explanation.**

---

# 13. GENERATIVE ANALOGIES

An analogy becomes powerful when it does more than decorate the message.

A useful analogy lets people transfer an existing mental model to a new problem.

### Structure

**Known system → shared relationship → unknown system**

Example:

> "Think of the database index like the index at the back of a book."

The analogy gives the learner an existing mental structure.

---

# 14. HIGH-CONCEPT COMMUNICATION

A high-concept description quickly communicates the essence of an unfamiliar idea by connecting it to something familiar.

Structure:

**New idea = familiar concept + meaningful difference**

This is useful for:

- product pitches
- startup explanations
- movies
- books
- software
- technical concepts

---

# 15. PRINCIPLE 2 — UNEXPECTED

## Core objective

**Get attention by violating expectations and maintain attention through curiosity.**

People constantly predict what will happen next.

Unexpectedness becomes useful when reality violates that prediction.

---

# 16. SURPRISE

Surprise can interrupt automatic attention.

A conventional statement:

> "Healthy eating matters."

Unexpected:

> "The food marketed as a movie-night snack can contain a surprisingly large amount of fat."

The second creates a prediction error.

### Attention mechanism

**Expectation → violation → surprise → attention**

---

# 17. SURPRISE IS NOT ENOUGH

A gimmick can produce immediate attention but no lasting learning.

Good unexpectedness should be:

**surprising + relevant + explainable**

Bad unexpectedness:

**surprising + irrelevant**

---

# 18. POSTDICTION

A strong surprise should become understandable after explanation.

This can be thought of as:

**"I didn't expect that — but now that I know the mechanism, it makes sense."**

That creates a satisfying learning experience.

### AI rule

When creating a surprising hook:

1. Establish an implied expectation.
2. Violate it.
3. Explain why.
4. Connect the explanation to the core idea.

---

# 19. CURIOSITY GAPS

Curiosity can be sustained by revealing that the audience knows less than they thought.

Structure:

**Known → missing piece becomes visible → desire to close gap → information → resolution**

Example:

> "Most people think X causes Y. But one overlooked factor changes the answer."

The audience now wants the missing factor.

---

# 20. CURIOSITY WITHOUT MANIPULATION

A curiosity gap should lead to a meaningful answer.

Avoid:

- fake mystery
- clickbait
- deliberately withholding obvious information
- misleading headlines

Ethical curiosity:

**Create a genuine information gap → satisfy it with useful information.**

---

# 21. PRINCIPLE 3 — CONCRETE

## Core objective

**Make ideas understandable by connecting them to things people can see, imagine, measure, or experience.**

Abstraction creates cognitive distance.

Concreteness reduces it.

---

# 22. ABSTRACT VS. CONCRETE

Abstract:

> "Improve quality."

Concrete:

> "Before submitting the report, verify every project ID against the source sheet."

The second specifies behavior.

---

# 23. CONCRETE LANGUAGE

Concrete communication uses:

- people
- objects
- actions
- numbers with context
- physical examples
- observable behavior
- specific situations

Instead of:

> "Increase productivity."

Use:

> "Reduce the manual review from 12 minutes to 5 minutes per record."

---

# 24. CONCRETE DOES NOT MEAN DUMBING DOWN

Technical information can remain technically accurate while becoming concrete.

For example:

Abstract:

> "The protocol has latency issues."

Concrete:

> "The request is sent at 10:02:14.220 and the response arrives at 10:02:14.890, creating approximately 670 ms of delay."

The second makes the issue observable.

---

# 25. VELCRO THEORY OF MEMORY

A useful metaphor from the book is that memories need attachment points.

Some information has many "hooks."

Concrete information can connect to:

- images
- experiences
- senses
- existing knowledge
- examples
- actions

Therefore:

**Concrete message → more mental hooks → easier retrieval**

---

# 26. SCHEMAS

People understand new information partly by fitting it into existing mental structures.

A schema is a familiar pattern for interpreting something.

Examples:

- restaurant
- airport
- classroom
- smartphone
- football match
- job interview

A communicator can make a new concept easier to understand by connecting it to an existing schema.

---

# 27. HUMAN-SCALE INFORMATION

Large numbers often fail to create intuitive understanding.

Instead of only providing a large statistic, translate it into a scale people can mentally represent.

Example:

**10 million units**

may be less meaningful than:

**If one person processed one unit every second, it would take months of continuous work.**

The goal is not to manipulate numbers.

The goal is to give the number a comprehensible frame.

---

# 28. PRINCIPLE 4 — CREDIBLE

## Core objective

**Give the audience reasons to believe the idea.**

Credibility can come from:

- authority
- evidence
- experience
- internal details
- demonstrations
- testable claims
- examples
- statistics
- trustworthy sources

---

# 29. EXTERNAL CREDIBILITY

External credibility relies on a source.

Examples:

- recognized expert
- institution
- certification
- independent study
- customer evidence
- professional authority

Useful when the audience lacks direct means to evaluate the claim.

---

# 30. INTERNAL CREDIBILITY

Internal credibility is built into the message itself.

The audience receives enough meaningful evidence to evaluate the claim.

For example:

Instead of:

> "This system is extremely fast."

Show:

**"In our test, the operation completed in 180 ms compared with 620 ms in the previous workflow."**

The evidence is contained in the claim.

---

# 31. DETAILS CAN CREATE CREDIBILITY

Specific details can signal that the communicator understands the subject.

But detail should serve the core.

Too many details produce:

- overload
- distraction
- confusion

Therefore:

**Use details that prove the important point.**

---

# 32. STATISTICS

Statistics are useful but can be psychologically weak when presented without context.

Raw:

> "43% improvement."

Better:

> "The average processing time dropped from 10 minutes to about 5.7 minutes."

The second provides a reference frame.

---

# 33. HUMAN-SCALE NUMBERS

A large number can become meaningful when converted into:

- time
- distance
- objects
- people
- everyday comparison
- frequency
- percentage of a known whole

### AI rule

Whenever a statistic feels abstract:

**Ask: "What would this look like in human terms?"**

---

# 34. THE SINATRA TEST

A single strong demonstration can sometimes establish credibility more effectively than many weak examples.

The underlying logic:

**If success in one highly relevant, difficult context demonstrates the capability, that example can carry disproportionate weight.**

Example:

If a product reliably solves a particularly difficult use case, that can serve as a credibility test.

---

# 35. TESTABLE CREDENTIALS

A useful credibility strategy is giving the audience a way to verify the claim.

Examples:

- public benchmark
- demonstration
- sample
- trial
- reproducible method
- independently verifiable result

This is stronger than asking the audience to trust the communicator blindly.

---

# 36. PRINCIPLE 5 — EMOTIONAL

## Core objective

**Make people care.**

Understanding is not enough.

People may understand an idea perfectly and still do nothing.

Emotion provides motivational energy.

---

# 37. WHY ABSTRACTIONS STRUGGLE

People often respond more strongly to identifiable individuals than to large abstractions.

Compare:

**"Millions of people are affected."**

with:

**"Meet one person whose daily routine changed because of the problem."**

The second gives emotion a specific target.

---

# 38. ASSOCIATION

An idea can become emotionally meaningful by connecting it to something the audience already values.

Possible values:

- family
- freedom
- security
- achievement
- fairness
- belonging
- pride
- identity
- independence
- curiosity

### Ethical requirement

The connection should be truthful.

---

# 39. SELF-INTEREST

People care about consequences for themselves.

Communicators can therefore explain:

- time saved
- money saved
- risk reduced
- convenience increased
- status improved
- skill gained
- pain avoided
- opportunity created

But self-interest is not the only source of motivation.

---

# 40. IDENTITY

One of the strongest emotional drivers can be identity.

People care about:

**Who I am.**

**Who I belong to.**

**Who I want to become.**

Examples:

- "I am an engineer."
- "We are a team that solves difficult problems."
- "I want to become a disciplined person."

Messages that align with identity can become more motivating.

---

# 41. EMOTION SHOULD SUPPORT THE IDEA

Emotion without substance can become manipulation.

A useful pattern is:

**Meaningful idea → relevant emotion → appropriate action**

not:

**Emotion → distraction from weak idea**

---

# 42. PRINCIPLE 6 — STORIES

## Core objective

**Use narrative to communicate information, enable mental simulation, and motivate action.**

Stories combine:

- people
- goals
- obstacles
- decisions
- consequences
- change

That structure makes abstract lessons easier to understand.

---

# 43. STORY AS MENTAL SIMULATION

A good story allows the listener to mentally rehearse:

**What happened → what decision was made → what obstacle appeared → what happened next**

This can prepare people for analogous situations.

---

# 44. THREE USEFUL STORY TYPES

The book identifies three broad story patterns useful for action:

## Challenge plot

A protagonist faces a difficult obstacle.

Useful for:

- persistence
- courage
- overcoming difficulty

## Connection plot

People from different backgrounds overcome separation.

Useful for:

- cooperation
- empathy
- community

## Creativity plot

A protagonist solves a problem through an unconventional idea.

Useful for:

- innovation
- experimentation
- problem solving

---

# 45. STORIES AS TEACHING TOOLS

A rule can be forgotten.

A story showing the rule in action can create a mental reference case.

Therefore:

**Principle + example story = stronger retrieval cue**

---

# 46. STORY STRUCTURE

A practical story skeleton:

1. Character
2. Goal
3. Situation
4. Obstacle
5. Decision
6. Consequence
7. Resolution
8. Lesson

Not every story needs all eight explicitly.

---

# 47. STORY VS. ANECDOTE

An anecdote is not automatically useful.

A sticky story should connect to:

- a principle
- a decision
- an emotional insight
- a behavior
- a lesson

Otherwise it may be entertaining but strategically irrelevant.

### 1. SIMPLE

### 2. UNEXPECTED

### 3. CONCRETE

### 5. EMOTIONAL

Before publishing a message, ask:

### Simple
- What is the one core idea?
- Could I state it in one sentence?
- Have I removed nonessential points?

### Unexpected
- What expectation am I challenging?
- Is there a genuine curiosity gap?
- Does the surprise serve the message?

### Concrete
- Can the audience visualize it?
- Are the examples specific?
- Are abstract claims translated into observable terms?

### Credible
- Why should the audience believe this?
- Is the evidence relevant?
- Can the claim be checked?

### Emotional
- Why should anyone care?
- What human value is affected?
- Is the emotional connection genuine?

### Stories
- Is there a story?
- Does it demonstrate the idea?
- Does it help the audience imagine action?

# 52. TECHNICAL COMMUNICATION

Technical experts frequently suffer from the Curse of Knowledge.

## Weak

> "The ECU has an intermittent CANFD communication fault due to a potential timing mismatch."

## Better

> "The ECU sometimes stops receiving the message. We see the failure after the timing changes, so the next test is to compare the message interval before and after the change."

The second preserves technical meaning while giving the listener a concrete model.

# 77. FAILURE MODES

## Failure 1 — Information dumping

Problem:

Everything is included.

Solution:

Prioritize the core.

## Failure 2 — Clickbait

Problem:

Attention is gained through misleading surprise.

Solution:

Use relevant, truthful unexpectedness.

## Failure 3 — Abstract language

Problem:

The audience cannot visualize the claim.

Solution:

Use people, objects, actions, and examples.

## Failure 4 — Authority without evidence

Problem:

"Experts say" becomes the entire argument.

Solution:

Add relevant evidence or demonstration.

## Failure 5 — Emotional manipulation

Problem:

Emotion substitutes for truth.

Solution:

Use genuine human stakes.

## Failure 6 — Story without lesson

Problem:

Interesting narrative, weak takeaway.

Solution:

Connect story directly to the core.

## Failure 7 — Expert language

Problem:

The communicator assumes too much prior knowledge.

Solution:

Audit the Curse of Knowledge.

# 78. THE "ONE SENTENCE TEST"

If you cannot answer:

**"What is the one thing I want them to remember?"**

the message probably has not been simplified enough.

---

# 79. THE "SHOW ME" TEST

For every abstraction ask:

**"What would this look like?"**

If you cannot answer, make the idea more concrete.

---

# 80. THE "WHY BELIEVE ME?" TEST

For every major claim ask:

**"What would make a skeptical person believe this?"**

Then provide evidence appropriate to the claim.

---

# 81. THE "WHY CARE?" TEST

For every major message ask:

**"Why does this matter to this audience?"**

Do not assume that the audience shares the communicator's priorities.

---

# 82. THE "WHAT WOULD THEY DO?" TEST

A message is incomplete if the audience understands it but does not know how to use it.

Ask:

**"If they remember this tomorrow, what should they do differently?"**

Then make that behavior explicit.

# 91. ETHICAL STICKINESS

Making an idea memorable increases its potential impact.

That creates responsibility.

A highly sticky falsehood can be more dangerous than a forgettable falsehood.

Therefore:

**Stickiness should amplify truth, usefulness, and legitimate value—not deception.**

### Ethical checklist

- Is the claim accurate?
- Is the evidence real?
- Is the surprise truthful?
- Is the emotional appeal proportionate?
- Is the story representative?
- Are important limitations disclosed?
- Am I using concreteness to clarify rather than distort?
- Am I creating curiosity to educate rather than trap?

# 95. FINAL SYNTHESIS

The deepest lesson of *Made to Stick* is:

**Important ideas do not become influential merely because they are correct. They must also survive the journey from one person's mind into another person's mind.**

That requires solving several communication problems:

- **Simplicity** solves overload.
- **Unexpectedness** solves inattention.
- **Concreteness** solves abstraction.
- **Credibility** solves disbelief.
- **Emotion** solves indifference.
- **Stories** solve the gap between knowing and doing.
- **Curiosity** keeps attention alive.
- **The Curse of Knowledge** reminds the communicator that expertise can become a communication obstacle.

The strongest communication therefore asks:

**What is the core?**

**Why will they notice it?**

**Can they picture it?**

**Why should they believe it?**

**Why should they care?**

**Can they imagine using it?**

**Will they remember it when the moment to act arrives?**

That is the central operating system of sticky ideas.

# 96. FINAL AI-READY SUMMARY

If an AI needs to remember this book in compact form:

> **Made to Stick communication model:** Make important ideas survive in memory by designing them rather than merely transmitting them. First identify the essential core and express it simply. Capture attention through relevant surprise or a genuine curiosity gap. Make abstract concepts concrete through people, actions, examples, demonstrations, and human-scale numbers. Establish credibility through relevant evidence, authority, testability, and concrete details. Make the audience care by connecting the idea to genuine self-interest, values, or identity. Use stories to provide mental simulation, emotional meaning, and examples of action. Continuously guard against the Curse of Knowledge by asking what the audience actually knows and can infer. Evaluate the final message by comprehension, attention, concreteness, credibility, emotional relevance, memory, and action.
